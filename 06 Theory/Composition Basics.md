# Composition Basics

Composition is how you arrange what's in the frame so the eye goes where you want. None of these are rules — they're tools. The masters broke every one of them on purpose, but they knew them first.

## Rule of thirds (and beyond)

Split the frame into a 3×3 grid. Place key subjects on the lines or where they cross. It works because it's off-centre — tension reads as more alive than dead-centre. Once it feels natural, graduate to **dynamic symmetry** and deliberate centring for stillness.

## Leading lines

Lines — roads, rails, shadows, railings, light beams — pull the eye through the frame toward your subject. Diagonals add energy; converging lines add depth. In Singapore, look for HDB corridors, void-deck columns, escalators, and overhead-bridge railings.

## Balance

Every element has visual "weight" (bright things, faces, sharp things, large things weigh more). A heavy subject on one side wants something to answer it on the other — even a small bright spot, or empty space. Unbalanced *can* be the point, but do it knowingly.

## Depth and layers

A 2D photo gains depth from **foreground / midground / background** layers. Shoot through a doorway, a railing, rain on glass, or a silhouetted figure in front of a lit wall. Layering is the heart of [[Saul Leiter]]'s work — reflections and glass stacking planes on top of each other.

## Edges and tangents

Watch the frame edges. A **tangent** is when two things just-touch (a head grazing a horizon line, a limb cut at the joint) — it looks like an accident. Either give clean separation or commit to a bold overlap. Cut limbs *between* joints, never *at* them.

## Negative space

Empty space isn't wasted — it gives the subject room to breathe and makes it feel small, lonely, or monumental. A single figure against a vast wall of shadow is the entire [[Fan Ho]] vocabulary.

## The [[Fan Ho]] geometry lesson

Fan Ho built photos out of pure geometry: a lone figure, a shaft of hard light cutting a diagonal, deep black shadow as a graphic shape, and a strong triangle or line organising it all. He didn't capture scenes — he *waited* for a person to walk into a composition he'd already framed. The shadow is a subject, not an absence. Study how he uses one bright wedge against heavy black, and how a tiny figure anchors a huge empty space.

## Practice mission

**"One subject, one shape."** Make ten frames where a single subject sits in deliberate negative space and one geometric element (a line, a triangle of light, a shadow edge) organises the frame. No clutter. Targets the **Composition** dimension. (See [[_Mission Library]].)
